# OpenPack v1: owner's audit (2026-11-01)

Audit of the closed v1 design at commit `6f0d592` (project wrap-up) plus the report branch `report-ib-ia`. Nothing was ever built. The owner's verdict was that the cost, above all the receiver end, is too high, and that the next iteration must start from cost. This document records how well v1 did against what it specified, why the cost ended where it did, what is reusable, and what must change for the next iteration (1–2 transmitters + 1 test receiver, scalable to 32+ mics, < 5 ms latency, cost first).

Supporting detail from the four audit passes is in `docs/audit/detail/` (cost, architecture, verification, market).

---

## 1. Verdict in one page

**Against its own spec, v1 did well.** Every numbered requirement in `SPEC.md` has a design answer and a virtual check (simulation, calculation or design-rule check). The firmware runs end to end in the BabbleSim radio simulator with CI gates on latency, loss, lock and clock drift. The pack is a complete, laid-out, DRC-clean board at $33.34 in parts. 22 ADRs and a 115-entry design log make every decision traceable.

**Against the owner's actual yardstick, v1 failed, and the failure was structural, not a part choice:**

| What the owner cared about | What v1 delivered | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Cheap receiver end | Rack £1,501.50 ex VAT (£1,190.94 without antenna stands), i.e. £149–188 per channel | 5–6× the per-pack cost per channel; no cost cap for the receiver end ever existed in the spec |
| Small, cheap receiver hardware | One 412 × 321.5 mm 4-layer board, £300 (estimated) for the board order alone, in a £140.76 Hammond 1U case | Half the board area is a mechanical tray for a vendor host board and 16 LEDs; 1U (ranked 9th) was honoured to the millimetre |
| Scales to 32 mics, no upper limit | FDMA, one 2 MHz carrier pair per pack, one receiver radio per pack | Hard ceiling of 13 packs per venue with no Wi-Fi, 4–8 with Wi-Fi; cost scales linearly (another rack per 8) |
| Self-configuring | Pack ID and carriers are compile-time Kconfig; channel plan is a hand-edited YAML hard-coded to 8 packs; RF scan is a stub; no downlink | Nothing is automatic |
| < 5 ms, target 3 ms | 2.67 ms by budget (2.29 ms with the DAC's low-latency filter); 4.6–5.0 ms with time diversity on | Met on paper; the radio part is simulated, the converter part is datasheet values |

**Root cause (process):** `REQUIREMENTS.md` has a pack cost target (R12, ~$30) but no receiver-end cost target. `SPEC.md` has PK-12 (pack BOM ≤ $35) and nothing for the rack. The £800–830 rack figure appears only in a late ticket brief (design log RK-7k). When the rack came in at £1,501 the agents wrote "reported as is" instead of stopping, because the hands-off rules only trigger a stop when a numbered requirement cannot be met, and no requirement covered the number that mattered most to the owner. Meanwhile R9 (1U rack, ranked 9th) and R1 (Wi-Fi survival, ranked 1st) were served exactly, and both pushed cost onto the receiver end.

**Root cause (technical):** two full copies of every frame on two fixed carriers, received by a single-channel nRF radio, means one receiver radio per pack, forever. Eight radios need a splitter; a splitter spread across eight flat mezzanines needs a 412 mm 4-layer board; UK power limits then push the remaining margin onto directional antennas on 4 m stands. Each step was the cheapest way to satisfy the step before it. The first step was never re-examined.

### 2.2 Timeline and effort

The whole project ran in roughly 36 hours of agent time (2026-10-31 15:00 to 2026-11-01), 66 merged PRs, 22 ADRs, about 3,600 lines of firmware C, 14,600 lines of Python (hardware generation, simulation, host, tests), three laid-out boards, two CadQuery enclosures. ### 2.3 The 18 open risks

Frozen in `docs/design-log.md`. The ones that matter for carrying knowledge forward:

- The R1 pass rests on a 0.086 % vs 0.1 % margin, an install rule, and a Wi-Fi model whose skirts are 5–8 dB kinder than the 802.11 mask allows (risk 15).
- Pack 1's 2482 MHz carrier was never simulated (bsim stops at 2480) and its legality rests on an estimated occupied bandwidth (risk 2).
- Two Fanstel module pin settings (ANT_SEL, MODE) are guesses (risk 4).
- Fanstel publishes no UK/EU declaration for either module (risk 5). Any UK product on these modules needs its own Declaration of Conformity.
- The codec choice (ADPCM-6) was made so a time-diversity copy fits in 85 bytes, then time diversity was turned off by default, and the choice was never re-opened (risk 13).

Three observations:

1. **The top five lines (PCB, stands, panels, case, far-wing coax) are £955, 64 % of the rack, and contain no semiconductors.** The entire RF and audio signal chain of the rack is under £150.
2. **Only £23.38 per channel is the receiver itself.** £88.63 per channel is fixed main board, host and case; £36.86 per channel is antennas and coax.
3. **£606 (40 %) is venue infrastructure** (antennas, coax, stands), a consequence of surviving a full house at the UK 9 dBm EIRP limit with non-hopping carriers, not of the receiver electronics. Reporting it inside "rack cost" hid that the electronics are ~£500.

### 3.2 Why the board is 412 × 321.5 mm

- **Width = 7 × 52 + 48 mm exactly.** The receiver mezzanine (48 × 90 mm) and its 52 mm slot pitch were set in the receiver layout file with the comment "No rack mechanics exist yet, so this defines them." Eight flat cards side by side fill the 19-inch width. Flat cards were chosen because vertical cards do not fit 44 mm (1U).
- **Depth = case inside depth minus 2.35 mm**: "one board filling the case." The rear 165 mm holds connectors, RF and receivers. **The front ~155 mm (48 % of the area) exists only to carry the CM4IO board's four mounting holes, two UART bridges and a row of 16 side-looking LEDs.**
- Spreading eight splitter ports over 400 mm made each Wilkinson tree ~830 mm of 50 Ω line and pushed its loss from 9 dB ideal to 13.66 dB, so the spec limit was raised to 14 dB to match. The board size set the splitter loss, not the other way round.
- The alternative ("narrow backplane strip plus a host tray joined by cables") was listed in the design log and never costed.

### 3.3 What same-architecture cost cuts were available and never taken

From the repo's own figures: shorten the board to ~170 mm (−£120 to −£180), generic steel case (−£80.76), sub-£30 host instead of CM4 + CM4IO (−£60 to −£90), venue booms instead of stands (−£311.66). Roughly £850–900 like for like, close to the £800–830 target, with no change to R1. The report's conclusion that "part swaps cannot fix the cost" is true only of the top five lines as a set.

**But same-architecture cuts do not solve the owner's problem**, because the architecture cannot scale (section 4) and still costs ~£60–100 per channel in electronics alone. They are listed so the next design does not repeat the mistakes, not as a rescue plan.

### 3.4 The pack

$33.34 at qty 100 excluding the cell (£25). The Fanstel BT40NE module is $13.47 of that (40 %), the PCM1840 ADC $3.37, the OPA1656 $1.84, the 4-layer board $2.50, the printed case $2.50. At Digi-Key UK module prices the BOM is ~$36.3. Assembly and conformal coating are excluded. The pack is the one piece of v1 hardware that is both complete and near its target, and it is largely independent of the air-interface change.

## 4. Why v1 cannot scale to 32 mics

(Spectrum math in `docs/audit/detail/architecture.md` §2.)

- Each pack owns a fixed pair of 2 MHz carriers, ≥ 40 MHz apart, and sends every 1 ms frame twice (copy A, copy B). Carriers must be ≥ 3 MHz apart (2 MHz neighbours collide in simulation).
- The UK band 2400–2483.5 MHz holds 27 such carriers: **13 dual-copy packs maximum with no Wi-Fi anywhere.** With Wi-Fi 1/6/11 present there are 8 gap carriers: 4 packs fully in the gaps, or the shipped compromise of 8 packs with one copy in a gap and one under Wi-Fi.
- One nRF5340 radio demodulates one channel at a time; a receive window costs ~360 µs, so one radio catches 2–3 copies per millisecond. **One receiver radio per pack is intrinsic to "two copies per frame on a single-channel radio."** That is the cost driver, and it is not a layout choice.
- Sharing a carrier pair between packs (TDMA) gives 3 packs per pair at 2 Mbps, 4–5 at 4 Mbps (nRF54L15), with no latency penalty, but needs a downlink beacon for slot sync, which v1 explicitly deferred (ADR-0004, one-way link).
- The rack adds cost linearly: 32 channels = 4 cases, 4 big boards, 4 CM4s, ~£5,100–7,200.

**Plain statement:** the v1 air interface tops out at 13 packs per venue, 8 under the Wi-Fi argument R1 depends on. Reaching 32 needs a different link design (section 6), not more racks.

## 5. Verification quality: what was real and what was theatre

(Per-SPEC-ID table, document disagreements and the test run are in `docs/audit/detail/verification.md`.)

### 5.1 What was real

- **The Python test suite passes:** 204 passed, 19 skipped, 0 failed (3 min 19 s). The skips are environmental on this machine (ngspice, ViSQOL/PESQ and trimesh not installed; 11 BabbleSim pseudo-terminal tests need a built firmware). CI on the final commits is green.
- **The firmware really runs in the radio simulator** with Wi-Fi interferers, and the scheduler, copy merge, time-diversity recovery, clock loops, codec and host API are verified there. Roughly 20 SPEC IDs are verified in simulation, 10 calculated, 20 design-only, 8 partial or claimed without an artefact.
- **Independent reviews found real bugs** that generated checks had passed: a filter with no RF path, a resistor that would burn out, a reversible connector putting 12 V on a radio pin, a frame skipped every 40 minutes.

### 5.2 What was theatre

1. **R1, the top requirement, was never gated in the shipped configuration.** The G2 gate (`tests/bsim/scenarios/run.py`) asserts the SYS-3 loss limit only when time diversity is on (`if ... and td:` at line 568), at the US 20 dBm EIRP default (line 679), with isotropic antennas and 5-second runs. The product ships with time diversity off (ADR-0017) at UK 9 dBm. The latest local G2 output shows the shipped default losing 0.2–0.65 % on packs 2–6 at 40 % Wi-Fi, and those rows are simply not asserted. `PLAN.md` and SPEC §9 still define the gate as "with time diversity on".
2. **The quoted 0.086 % margin is one 20-second run per case**, 17 lost frames in 19.7 k. The 95 % confidence interval is 0.046–0.132 %; the probability of seeing 17 or fewer if the true rate were exactly the 0.1 % limit is 0.32. SYS-3 specifies one hour (3.6 million frames). The run's result files are not committed; only the geometry inputs are.
3. **The project's own burst-loss model contradicts the pass.** `sim/loss/RESULTS.md` says time-diversity-off never passes at ≥ 0.1 % busy on a gap carrier; the simulator's 0.000 % rests on the gap carriers being almost perfectly clean (probe loss ≤ 0.006). The Wi-Fi busy fractions are literature guesses marked `estimate: true`; the venue occupancy scan was dropped.
4. **Not modelled at all:** Bluetooth, hotspots off channels 1/6/11, 40 MHz Wi-Fi, body loss (10–20 dB), fading, moving actors, receiver compression, pack 1's 2482 MHz carrier (the simulator stops at 2480), and the eighth pack. The receiver's antenna-switching logic (RX-5) was replayed in Python, not run in firmware. The Wi-Fi spectral skirts in the model are 5–8 dB kinder than the 802.11 mask allows.
5. **The R1 acceptance setup was changed until it passed.** ADR-0003's acceptance geometry was replaced by ADR-0021's modelled theatre without superseding ADR-0003.
6. **Latency: only 885 of 2,670 µs was measured** (the radio part, in bsim). The rest is datasheet group delay. BabbleSim executes code in zero time, so the "encode + IPC 100 µs" line is a deadline, not a measurement. The headline "2.28 ms as built" and the TD-on figure of ≤ 4.6 ms (with 4 µs of margin in the gate) both depend on the PCM5102A's low-latency IIR filter, whose effect on audio quality was never checked.
7. **Documents disagree with each other:** SYS-2 latency is given as 2.65, 2.67 and 2.28 ms in different places; the rack noise figure is 11.5 dB in SPEC and 12.86 dB in the report; `audit.py` has a 130-line block duplicated verbatim; `hw/REVIEW.md` says both that the JLC BOM has and lacks LCSC numbers; REQUIREMENTS still says "US first"; gate G4 (purchase) was never actually passed (no fab DFM run); PK-13 (boot ≤ 1 s) has no test at all.

### 5.3 Honest judgement on R1

A real theatre would not behave like the model. The margins quoted are within the noise of the simulation, the simulated configuration is not the one that ships, and every physical input (bodies, movement, real Wi-Fi spectra, module pin settings, converter delays) was deferred to a bench phase that never ran. The correct reading of v1's R1 status is "plausible on a clean gap carrier, unknown otherwise", not "pass".

### 5.4 Lessons

- A simulation gate must run the configuration that ships.
- Report margins with confidence intervals and run lengths; a sub-percent margin from 20 seconds of simulation is not evidence.
- Do not change the acceptance setup to get a pass without superseding the ADR that defined it.
- Measure the real hardware early. Three boards were laid out before any datasheet delay, module pin or Wi-Fi assumption had been checked on a bench.

## 6. Technology directions for the next iteration

(Web research with sources and UK prices in `docs/audit/detail/market.md`, fetched 2026-11-01. Prices are qty-1 and change; verify before buying.)

### 6.1 What the market says

- **Nobody sells a 32-channel 2.4 GHz mic system.** Consumer kits (Rode, DJI, Hollyland, Boya, Saramonic) cost £19–85 per transmitter but co-locate only 3–5 sets per room (6–10 mics). Pro 2.4 GHz tops out at 20 channels (Audio-Technica System 20 PRO). The only 32-in-one-room systems are UHF / 1.4 GHz wideband TDMA (Sennheiser Spectera, 64 channels at ~1 ms; Shure Axient Digital, 2 ms) at thousands of pounds per channel. The owner's target sits in a gap no product fills, which is both the opportunity and the warning.
- **Every consumer 2.4 GHz mic is a 2 Mbps GFSK (Bluetooth-PHY-class) radio**, the same class v1 used. Confirmed nRF5340-based designs exist (AnkerWork M650, Virscient LiveOnAir with a sub-5 ms claim). The parts are not the problem; the air interface and the receiver architecture are.
- **UK regulation moved in the project's favour on 29 April 2026:** 1785–1805 MHz became licence-exempt for audio PMSE (50 mW EIRP body-worn, 20 mW otherwise; IR 2030/35/3, S.I. 2026/396). That is 20 MHz of spectrum with no Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or phones in it, 2.5× the power v1 could use. The catch: no cheap transceiver silicon tunes there, so it needs a mixer front-end or an SDR. Verify the SI text on legislation.gov.uk before relying on it.
- 2.4 GHz stays capped at ~10 dBm for a continuous non-hopping carrier (10 dBm/MHz PSD rule), and below 10 dBm there is no duty-cycle or listen-before-talk requirement. Adaptive frequency hopping (≥ 15 hops) allows 20 dBm EIRP.

### 6.3 Two things to keep in mind

1. **Capacity is a receiver problem, not a transmitter problem.** A 2 Mbps radio measures ~1.7 Mbps of real throughput; 32 packs × ~64 kbps of 6-bit audio is 2.05 Mbps plus overhead. One radio cannot hear 32 packs. Four to eight radios can, for under £100. That is the whole difference between v1's £1,500 rack and a credible target.
2. **The downlink is the enabling feature**, not an optional v2 extra. Slot assignment, hop sync, join, and self-configuration all need the rack to talk to the packs. v1 reserved a 300 µs slot for it and never used it. The next prototype should build the beacon first and the audio second.

### 7.2 Decisions not to carry forward

- One receiver radio per pack; static carrier pair per pack; two full copies on two fixed carriers; fixed gap carriers including UK-only 2482 MHz.
- One-way link with the downlink deferred. Self-configuration needs the downlink from day one; keep the 300 µs slot idea.
- Receive-only rack chain (LNA → BAW → 1:8 Wilkinson on a 412 mm 4-layer board); CM4 + CM4IO host with 8 UARTs; Hammond 1U case; "fill the case" board sizing.
- Wing panel antennas on 4 m stands as the way to meet R1 at 9 dBm. Hopping removes the dependence on gap carriers and lifts the legal power.
- ADPCM-6 chosen only so a TD copy fits 85 bytes.
- Designing three PCBs before anything had run on silicon (R13 taken to the extreme, ADR-0019 single purchase).

### 7.3 Firmware gaps to know about before a bench demo

- `#132`: console `id`/`chan`/`power`/`td` are saved but do not retune the radio; each pack is built with its own Kconfig.
- `#128`: host DFU still streams raw bytes; firmware expects MCUboot SMP.
- Receiver `scan` is a stub returning −100 dBm.
- DK build: the I2S fallback pin P0.20 is the nRF5340 DK's UART TX, and P0.23/P0.24 are its buttons; needs an `i2s0` pinctrl overlay and `CONFIG_OP_FEM_NRF21540=n`.
- Net core image is not under MCUboot; USB CDC and DFU are build-verified only.

### 7.4 Minimal bench prototype from the existing design (for reference)

Two nRF5340 DKs (pack and receiver roles exist in the build), a PCM5102A breakout for audio out, a PCM1840 EVM or WAV-in-bsim for audio in, laptop as host. Roughly £295 with two pack boards built, ~£640 with the full bench kit (logic analyser, tinySA, PPK2). This demonstrates codec, latency, lock, merge, PLC and the clock loops, but with one pack per receiver, which shows the scaling wall immediately.

## 8. Process lessons for the next iteration

1. **Put the receiver-end cost per channel in the requirements as a gate, ranked where the owner ranks it (first).** v1 had no such number, so £1,501 could not fail anything.
2. **Design for N channels from day one.** The channel-count ceiling of the air interface belongs in the spec next to the latency number.
3. **Cost board area and enclosure as line items with owners.** The main board went £25 → £40 → £300 across two PRs and the project closed on an unquoted estimate.
4. **Treat low-ranked form-factor wishes as preferences, not tolerances.** 1U drove the mezzanine shape, the board width, the splitter loss and the case price.
5. **Mount vendor boards to the chassis, never to the RF backplane.**
6. **Build and measure early.** Three PCBs were laid out before a single frame had crossed the air on real silicon; two module pin settings and both converter delays are still guesses. The next iteration should run on dev kits first, measure latency with a logic analyser, and only then draw a board.
7. **Stop-and-ask when a lower-ranked requirement starts driving the cost of a higher-ranked one**, even if no numbered requirement is formally violated.
8. **Separate venue infrastructure from product cost in every report.**
